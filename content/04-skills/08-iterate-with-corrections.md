# Iterate with corrections

A skill is not finished when it is written. It is finished when it has
been wrong once and been corrected.

How much correcting depends entirely on how wide the job is.

## Narrow jobs work straight away

If there is exactly one right answer, the skill will get it and keep
getting it. *How to write a caption in the SDNY* is one right answer. So
is *how to lay out our engagement letter*, or *how we number exhibits*.
Write it once, from examples, and it will be right on Monday and still
right in March.

These are worth building precisely because they are boring. Nobody wants
to hold the house exhibit convention in their head, and now nobody has to.

## Broad jobs need a few rounds

Two kinds of job need iteration. The **broad** one — *how to write
motions* — where the work varies enormously from matter to matter. And
the one with **several correct answers** — *how to write a caption in
federal court*, where every district is right in its own way and the skill
has to know which one it is in.

Give it your SDNY examples and ask for a filing in the Northern District
of California, and it will hand you a perfectly formed SDNY caption. It is
not confused. It learned one answer, and you never told it the answer
varies.

## How to patch it

When it gets one wrong, do not rewrite the skill and do not explain the
rule. Go back to the same session, or open one where the skill can be
seen, and do two things:

1. **Say what went wrong.** Plainly. *"That caption is the SDNY format.
   This filing is going to the Northern District of California."*
2. **Show it the other answer.** Give it a real caption from that court —
   the actual document, not your description of it.

Then ask it to patch the skill. From the two examples side by side it can
work out three things at once: what never changes, what varies and with
what, and — the valuable one — **what it should ask you about when it
cannot tell.** A skill that stops and asks which court you are in is
worth far more than one that guesses correctly most of the time.

The same loop covers the rest of it. It used the wrong deadline because
one court counts days differently — show it both. It wrote a pre-motion
letter where that practice does not exist — show it a matter where you
simply filed. It used a federal docket number in a state caption — show it
the index number. Each round makes the skill less about one court and more
about how you decide.

Two or three passes is normal on a broad skill. After that it will mostly
be right, and when it is not, you already know what to do.

## And then this stops being your job

> finding where it fails, patching it and testing again. Automatically, on

That is some way off for you. It is worth knowing it exists, because it
means the correcting you do now is the last version of the work that is
