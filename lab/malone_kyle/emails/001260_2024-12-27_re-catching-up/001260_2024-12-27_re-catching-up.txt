>>> Evening Kyle:
>>>
>>> Good to hear from you. Hope all is well on your end. And happy to help.
>>>
>>> There are basically two answers to your question:
>>>
>>>
>>>    1. The quickest way to get Caren and Azi here would be a tourist
>>>    (B-2) visa. But it would not allow Caren to work without going through a
>>>    process with USCIS to change that status to someone else with a work
>>>    permit. And the filing fees for USCIS are quite expensive. So it would get
>>>    them in the country the quickest, but then there would be more expense to
>>>    get longer stays and work permission.
>>>
>>>
>>>
>>>    2. The way to get work permission and long stays in Caren’s case
>>>    would really be an E-1 visa. The E-1 can be obtained directly at the
>>>    consulate—without going through USCIS before or after. It comes with
>>>    permission to work, a visa that lasts for 5 years, a stay that lasts for 2
>>>    years at a time (you just have to take a trip overseas for a day every 2
>>>    years to reset it), and perpetual renewals—meaning you could technically
>>>    live here on that visa forever. But most people use that visa to upgrade to
>>>    an EB-2 or EB-3 green card, which take 1 or 2 years to get, down the road.
>>>
>>>
>>> There are other visas that do something similar, like the E-2 and the
>>> L-1. But the E-2 requires investment, so that will cost you a decent chunk
>>> of change. And the L-1 has to go through USCIS first, at considerable cost
>>> and delay.
>>>
>>> The E-1 is really a hidden gem of immigration. I say hidden because it
>>> is not available to every country: only a few dozen on this list
>>> <https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/visa-information-resources/fees/treaty.html>,
>>> including the Philippines. It is not quite as simple as a tourist visa, but
>>> it is not too complicated either. In a nutshell, the requirements are as
>>> follows:
>>>
>>>
>>>    1. Caren needs to have a passport from a country on that list, such
>>>    as the Philippines, which I presume she does.
>>>
>>>
>>>
>>>    2. We file an application for Caren and Azi at the consulate in
>>>    Manila, which gets the process going.
>>>
>>>
>>>
>>>    3. We prepare an E visa application packet showing that Caren has a
>>>    plan that meets the E-1 requirements. That plan needs to show that, once in
>>>    the United States, Caren will engage in some sort of trade with the
>>>    Philippines. Here is what that looks like:
>>>
>>>
>>>
>>>    1. Trade can be anything, products or services. It can be Caren
>>>       “importing” services by managing freelance workers from the Philippines
>>>       that work for U.S. clients, or it can be Caren “exporting” products by
>>>       buying things in the United States and shipping them to paying customers in
>>>       the Philippines.
>>>
>>>       2. Trade does not have to be massive. What the government wants
>>>       to see is a plan that shows there will be more than one transaction.
>>>
>>>       3. Trade does not have to have started already. The law
>>>       specifically allows people to get this visa with prospective transactions.
>>>
>>>
>>> If this sounds too far-fetched, it is not. Here are some examples of
>>> E-1’s I got approved this year:
>>>
>>>
>>>    1. A client from a country where consumer electronics (think iPhones
>>>    and similar) are too expensive got an E-1 visa to buy those things at Best
>>>    Buy and then ship them to his home country for a profit. The total number
>>>    of shipments under the guy’s belt was 4.
>>>
>>>
>>>
>>>    2. A client from a country famous for its textiles got an E-1 for
>>>    having her family business “sell” her some dresses, ship them via FedEx to
>>>    the U.S., and let her resell them here. Each dress went for less than $100.
>>>
>>>
>>>
>>>    3. A client who was a freelance coder overseas got an E-1 for coming
>>>    to the U.S. and working from here instead. All he had to do was get a
>>>    subcontractor here, and the work done in the U.S. to a client in his home
>>>    country counted as a “service exported.”
>>>
>>>
>>> And so on. The point is that, if you are looking for speed, safety, and
>>> relative affordability, I would say the E-1 is the way to go. Again, you
>>> get most benefits of a green card while waiting for a green card.
>>>
>>> In terms of actual cost and timetables, you can expect the following:
>>>
>>>
>>>    1. Step 1 is to file the application at the consulate. This is the
>>>    immediate step to get the ball rolling and the clock ticking: the rest we
>>>    can prep after we file, and at least the process runs in parallel. For this
>>>    step, you can expect costs to be around *$1,150*: $650 goes to the
>>>    government for the filing fees ($315 per person); $500 to cover one hour of
>>>    my time (I will not charge for my team’s time in doing the heavy lifting
>>>    with the forms).
>>>
>>>
>>>
>>>    2. Step 2 is to prep the plan for the interview at the consulate—and
>>>    to prep Caren for the interview. For this step, you can expect costs to be
>>>    around another *$1,000*, or two hours of my time. It will probably
>>>    take me longer than that, but that is fine: I do not need to bill for every
>>>    hour I work.
>>>
>>>
>>>
>>>    3. Step 3 is for Caren to attend the interview. There is no
>>>    additional charge here. But the issue is timing. Unlike other visas, where
>>>    you book your appointment in advance, with the E visas you have to wait for
>>>    the consulate to open a spot for you—where they can have someone that can
>>>    review the more complex application. So it could be *2-3 months *after
>>>    filing the application before the consulate invites Caren—at which point
>>>    she can book soon or much later. Which is why the sooner you do step 1, the
>>>    better: there is only upside to already being “in line.”
>>>
>>>
